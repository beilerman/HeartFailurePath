# Policy questions for the panel

These are choices of judgement, not measurement. Each is marked as policy inside the tool. Please answer the ones you have a view on. A short answer is enough. Your answers will be collected across reviewers and applied once.

## A. How the factors are weighed

1. **The six weights.** Death 30, heart failure hospitalization 22, side effects 14, cost to the patient 14, symptoms 10, dosing burden 10. If you had 100 points to distribute, how would you distribute them? The entry form also lets the clinician choose what matters most to the patient: Balanced, Prioritize treatment evidence (the weights of cost and dosing burden are halved), Keep cost low (the weight of cost is tripled), or Keep the regimen simple (the weight of dosing burden is tripled), with all six weights then rescaled to total 100%. Are those the right alternatives, and the right sizes?
2. **Two new drug classes per visit.** By default the tool starts at most two new drug classes at one visit (the clinician can change this limit, from 0 to 6, on the entry form). A voluntary switch from an ACE inhibitor or ARB to sacubitril/valsartan counts as one. Dose increases of drugs already taken, and changes forced by a safety rule, do not count. Is two the right default?
3. **Ties go to no change.** When the best option scores within one point (of 100) of leaving the regimen unchanged, "No medication change" is listed first and the others are shown as alternatives. In the tool's 1,032 reference runs this rule decides the first option in 150 (about one in seven), nearly always putting "raise spironolactone from 25 to 50 mg" or "add vericiguat" second. Is that the right way to present a change the evidence cannot distinguish from no change?
4. **The exception for congestion.** The tool does not model congestion relief. So when congestion is recorded and the best option includes a diuretic step, that option stays first even if it scores within one point of no change, and the screen says why. Reasonable?
5. **Unresolved safety warnings.** An option that carries a counted safety warning loses 8 points per warning. Most are clinical cautions (for example sacubitril/valsartan at a systolic pressure under 100); a few are procedural notes such as a drug-level check or an unknown price. Too much, too little, or the wrong approach?

## B. Where the tool departs from usual practice

6. **Beta-blocker in atrial fibrillation.** For a patient with reduced ejection fraction in atrial fibrillation who is not on a beta-blocker, the tool usually does not put a beta-blocker in its first option. The reason is the published pooled figure for death: hazard ratio 0.97 (0.83 to 1.14) in atrial fibrillation, against 0.73 (0.67 to 0.80) in sinus rhythm in the same pooled analysis. For a patient not in atrial fibrillation the tool uses 0.66, the pool of CIBIS-II, MERIT-HF and COPERNICUS, which enrolled all rhythms. The tool shows a rate-control alert but has no rate-control step. Should the tool use this figure as it stands?
7. **Semaglutide ahead of an SGLT2 inhibitor at ejection fraction above 40.** In an obese patient this can happen, driven by the larger symptom effect. Acceptable?
8. **Hydralazine/isosorbide dinitrate.** It may be absent from the first option for a self-identified Black patient with reduced ejection fraction when other additions score higher under the two-change limit. Acceptable for a first option, given it is shown as eligible?
9. **Removing an established MRA below the label's kidney limit**, while a renin-angiotensin drug at the same kidney function is left in place and may be raised (an alert appears only below an eGFR of 20). Is that asymmetry right?
10. **Carvedilol and fainting.** The label gives two figures for fainting in the same trial: 2.9% against 2.3% on placebo in its warnings section, an excess of 0.6 points, and 8% against 5% in its adverse-event table, an excess of 3 points. Neither is scaled to a year. The tool charges 0.6. Which would you use?

## C. Definitions

11. **Acute decompensation.** The tool reads it as NYHA class IV, or class III with more than 2 kg above dry weight and at least two congestion findings. It then withholds beta-blocker starts and increases and, when congestion is also recorded and an oral loop diuretic start or increase is allowed, adds that step to every option. The urgent same-day alert appears only when a weight more than 1 kg above dry weight, a congestion finding or a sign of poor perfusion is also recorded; NYHA class IV alone raises a prompt to review the symptoms and consider an advanced heart failure evaluation. Is the definition too broad or too narrow?
12. **Poor perfusion.** Low output (ejection fraction below 20 with at least two of: cool extremities, pulse pressure 25 or less, BUN to creatinine ratio above 20), or acute decompensation with cool extremities, a systolic pressure under 90, or a pulse pressure of 25 or less. Nothing is started or increased except a loop diuretic for congestion and a replacement for a beta-blocker that must stop. A systolic pressure under 90 on its own stops the review with alerts only. At an ejection fraction of 20 or more, two or more of the three markers raise a "possible poor perfusion" alert, and each option that starts or raises a drug carries a warning to assess perfusion first; scores and order are unchanged. Agree?
13. **Congestion.** One congestion finding, or more than 1 kg above dry weight, counts as congested. Volume depletion needs more than 1 kg below dry weight and no congestion finding. A new loop diuretic is started only when the weight is more than 1 kg above the dry weight; a congestion finding without that weight gain does not start one, though it can support raising an existing one. With two or more findings, no weight gain and no loop diuretic taken, an alert says so and asks the clinician to check the dry weight. Agree?
14. **Pregnancy status.** For a female patient younger than 56, or of unknown age, pregnancy status must be entered. If it is missing, drugs unsafe in pregnancy are withheld and the results usually show no medication change (with the age also missing, only a partial review with no options). Is 56 the right age?
15. **Out-of-scope conditions.** The tool shows no ranked options when any of these is recorded: cardiac amyloidosis; hypertrophic or restrictive cardiomyopathy; severe aortic stenosis or other severe valve disease awaiting intervention; congenital heart disease; active cardiotoxic cancer treatment; transplant or ventricular assist device; dialysis. Anything to add or remove? (The trials also excluded, for example, myocarditis, constrictive pericarditis, type 1 diabetes for the SGLT2 inhibitors, and a recent myocardial infarction; these are not on the list.)

## D. Thresholds that change the first option

A one-unit change in an entered value across any of these changes the first option in a substantial share of patients. About two thirds come from drug labels, trial entry criteria or guidelines; those marked "tool policy" do not. Please mark any you would move, and any you think should be graded instead of a cut-off.

How the table was made: 800 constructed patients per row, all with stable vital signs, potassium 4.4, eGFR 62, ejection fraction 30 with an earlier ejection fraction of 40 or below on record, NYHA class II and 2.5 kg above dry weight, on a random current regimen, with commercial insurance prices. The base is otherwise a 65-year-old man, 120/70 mmHg, heart rate 72, sinus rhythm, NT-proBNP 2000, BMI 32, creatinine 1.2, a recent worsening event recorded, no iron studies or hemoglobin recorded, no allergies, comorbidities or other drugs, and a budget of 4000. The random regimen takes each drug class independently with the probabilities used by the review's patient generators (an ACE inhibitor, ARB or sacubitril/valsartan 55%, beta-blocker 55%, steroidal MRA or finerenone 35%, SGLT2 inhibitor 35%, loop diuretic 50%, and so on), a random listed dose and frequency, all confirmed as taken. One value is moved by one unit across the threshold and nothing else changes; the same regimen is used on both sides. Exceptions, each stated in its row: the hemoglobin row also records iron deficiency (ferritin 60, transferrin saturation 15); the NYHA row records two congestion findings (raised jugular venous pressure, orthopnea). "First option changes" is the share of those patients whose first option differs on the two sides. "No drug in common" is the share whose two first options share no drug. A side that shows no option counts as different from a side that shows one, and two sides that both show none count as the same. The shares describe this base patient and would differ for others. For the age row, pregnancy status was left unrecorded. With cash prices the three ejection-fraction rows give: LVEF 39 to 40: 11%; LVEF 40 to 41: 11%; LVEF 49 to 50: 0%.

## E. Further choices we would like your view on

16. **Diuretic not in the first option.** In some congested patients the first option contains no diuretic step and the diuretic appears in the second or third option, because the tool has no measured outcome figure for congestion relief and other changes score higher. Acceptable, or should a congested patient's first option always include a diuretic step?
17. **Stopping a loop diuretic.** When a patient is below dry weight, or at dry weight with no congestion finding, an option may stop the loop diuretic outright instead of lowering the dose, because the side-effect estimate favours it. This can be the first option, including in NYHA class III or IV and in a low-output state. Would you require a stepwise reduction?
18. **Two new classes during acute decompensation.** Apart from beta-blockers, the two-per-visit limit is not tightened when the acute decompensation alert is raised. Should it be?
19. **Sacubitril/valsartan at an eGFR of 20 to 30.** The tool allows a start or increase in this range; the monitoring plan asks for a kidney dosing review and a reduced starting dose. Agree?
20. **Low oxygen saturation.** A saturation below 90 raises an urgent alert but does not by itself count as decompensation, so options may still start a beta-blocker. Should it?
21. **Allergy to a related drug.** An option that starts a drug of the same class as a listed allergy carries a warning to confirm the reaction first. No such warning is raised for an ARB after an allergy to an ACE inhibitor, or for an ACE inhibitor or an ARB other than valsartan after an allergy to sacubitril/valsartan, unless angioedema is recorded. Valsartan after an allergy to sacubitril/valsartan does carry the warning, and semaglutide and tirzepatide are treated as one family, so an allergy to either puts the warning on the other. Is that the right line?
22. **Advanced heart failure alert.** One recorded stop of a foundational drug for intolerance counts as a marker, which is broader than "progressive intolerance". Too sensitive?
23. **Digoxin dose and kidney function.** The tool lowers the digoxin ceiling to 62.5 micrograms only below an eGFR of 30, while the label doses by lean body weight and creatinine clearance. Adequate for a tool that leaves an established dose unchanged otherwise?
24. **Congestion on examination at dry weight.** A patient already on a loop diuretic, at dry weight, with congestion findings gets no prompt to reconsider the dry weight, however many findings are recorded (an increase of the loop diuretic may still be offered). The prompt appears only when no loop diuretic is taken and the findings reach the count used for decompensation. Should there be one?
25. **When nothing fits the budget.** The tool then shows up to three of the five least costly safe options, marks each as over budget, and lists them by score, or from the lowest monthly cost when "Keep cost low" is selected; the screen says which. Is that the right way to show them?

26. **Raising a loop diuretic without recorded congestion.** A loop diuretic increase is held only at or below dry weight, so a patient up to 1 kg above dry weight with no congestion finding may be offered an increase (between 1% and 3% of the loop increases the tool offers in random samples); the option says why. Should an increase require a congestion finding or a weight more than 1 kg above dry weight?

27. **Beta-blocker after recorded bronchospasm.** A recorded bronchospasm with a beta-blocker blocks a new start of a non-selective one, but carvedilol already taken is continued and may be raised. Should an established non-selective beta-blocker be switched or held?

28. **Furoscix as the only loop diuretic.** The label describes Furoscix as a short course beside an oral loop diuretic. For a congested patient who takes Furoscix and no oral loop diuretic, every option stops Furoscix and starts an oral loop diuretic at the usual starting dose (for example furosemide 20 mg once daily), the same start offered to a patient who takes no loop diuretic. No sentence says that the diuretic dose falls. Should Furoscix be kept until the oral drug is established, or the oral start be higher?

29. **Replacing a loop diuretic when output is low.** With poor perfusion the tool starts no drug and raises no dose, with one exception besides a diuretic step for congestion: a loop diuretic that must stop (for example for a recorded allergy) is replaced by another when congestion is recorded. The replacement drug may differ from the one offered to the same patient with normal perfusion. Is a replacement right in that state, or should the tool only advise urgent assessment?
